Those three things have something in common: they are all high in histamine.
Wine and aged cheese are fermented, and leftovers accumulate histamine as they
sit. If you react to all of them, the issue is usually not any single food. It
is that histamine is arriving faster than your body can break it down.

## Think of it as a bucket

You are not allergic to these foods. You have a clearance capacity, and when
intake exceeds it, the overflow shows up as headache, flushing, a racing heart,
itching, congestion or anxiety that seems to come from nowhere.

That explains the maddening inconsistency. The same glass of wine is fine in
January and gives you a migraine in March, because in March the bucket was
already most of the way full before you poured.

## Where the gut comes in

The enzyme that clears histamine from food is produced along the lining of your
small intestine. Two things can undermine it:

- damage to that lining, which reduces how much enzyme you make
- certain gut bacteria, which produce histamine themselves and add to the load

So a gut in poor shape lowers your capacity and raises your input at the same
time. This is why histamine issues so often appear after a course of
antibiotics, a gastrointestinal infection, or a long stretch of stress. Nothing
changed about the wine.

## Working the right end

A low-histamine diet brings relief, and it is a reasonable place to start. But
it manages input while leaving capacity untouched, and a permanently narrowed
diet is its own kind of problem. The durable work is on the lining and on the
bacterial population producing histamine in the first place.

Knowing whether you are carrying histamine-producing strains changes that from a
theory about your diet into something you can act on.
