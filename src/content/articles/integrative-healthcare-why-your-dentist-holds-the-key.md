Because the two professions were formally separated about 150 years ago and have
operated as different fields, with different schools, different licensing and
different insurance, ever since. It was an administrative decision, not a
biological one. Your mouth did not get the memo.

## What the split cost

The consequence is strange when you say it plainly. The clinician who examines
your soft tissue, your bone, your inflammation levels and your breathing pattern
two or three times a year has no routine channel to tell the clinician managing
your chronic disease what they observed. Two separate charts. Two separate
appointments. No shared record.

Meanwhile the mouth is a genuinely useful early window. Gum tissue registers
systemic inflammation. Enamel erosion can point to reflux or to an eating
disorder. Recession and bone loss track with diabetes. Jaw shape and tongue
position relate directly to whether you can breathe while you sleep.

## What your dentist is positioned to notice first

They are frequently the first clinician to spot signs of:

- inflammatory load showing up in gum and bone before it shows up in bloodwork
- reflux, from the specific pattern of erosion on the back of the teeth
- an airway problem, from the shape of the palate and the wear on the teeth
- grinding that points to sleep-disordered breathing rather than to stress

None of that requires new technology. It requires someone to ask.

## What integrative actually means here

It is a much less exotic idea than the word suggests. It means the dentist asks
about your systemic conditions and your medications, and means it. It means your
physician knows whether you have active gum disease. It means when something in
the mouth suggests something elsewhere, there is a referral instead of a shrug.

Nothing about this requires you to wait for the professions to reconcile. You
can carry the information across yourself, and it helps a great deal if what you
carry is a result rather than an impression.
