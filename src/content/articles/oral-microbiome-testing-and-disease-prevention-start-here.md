It tells you which bacterial species are living in your mouth and roughly how
much of each. That sounds modest until you consider the alternative, which is
what dentistry has done for a century: look at the damage, guess at the cause,
and treat the damage.

## What a dental exam sees, and what it misses

An exam and an x-ray show you outcomes. A cavity that already formed. Bone that
has already receded. Tissue that is already inflamed. All real, all useful, all
late. By the time an x-ray shows bone loss, the process that caused it has been
running for years.

A saliva sample looks at the cause layer instead. It answers a different
question: not what has happened, but what is currently living here and what that
population tends to do.

## What you get back

A sequencing panel identifies the organisms present and their relative
abundance, which lets you see things an exam cannot:

- whether acid-producing species that dissolve enamel are overrepresented
- whether species associated with gum and bone breakdown have established
- whether the beneficial species that keep your mouth's pH stable are present at all
- a baseline you can compare against a year from now

That last one matters more than people expect. A single reading is a snapshot. A
second reading is a direction.

## The honest limits

This is not a diagnosis and it does not replace an exam. Bacterial populations
shift with diet, sleep, medication and stress, so a result describes a period
rather than a permanent identity. And the science connecting specific oral
strains to specific systemic outcomes is still developing. Some associations are
well established, others are promising and not yet settled.

What it does give you is a reason to act before there is damage to point at, and
something concrete to re-measure once you have changed anything.
