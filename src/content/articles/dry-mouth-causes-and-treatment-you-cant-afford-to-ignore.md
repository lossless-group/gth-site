Usually one of three things: a medication you are taking, breathing through your
mouth overnight, or dehydration you have not noticed because it happens while
you sleep. All three are common and all three are fixable. What is not fine is
leaving it alone, because saliva is doing considerably more work than keeping
your mouth comfortable.

## What saliva is actually for

It buffers acid, so the pH in your mouth recovers after you eat. It carries
minerals that repair enamel in its earliest stage of breakdown. It contains
antimicrobial proteins that keep certain species in check. And it physically
rinses.

Take that away and the environment changes character quickly. Acid lingers.
Repair stalls. The species that thrive in a dry, acidic mouth gain ground over
the ones that do not. People with chronic dry mouth can develop decay at a rate
that has nothing to do with how carefully they brush, which is one of the more
demoralizing things I see in practice.

## The usual causes, in order

Medication is the most common by far, and the list is long: antihistamines,
antidepressants, blood pressure drugs, diuretics, anything with an
anticholinergic effect. If you take several, the effects stack.

Then mouth breathing, which dries the mouth for six or eight hours a night and
is often a sign of a nasal or airway problem worth investigating on its own.
Then dehydration, alcohol, caffeine, and sometimes an autoimmune condition
affecting the salivary glands.

## What helps

Sipping water all day treats the symptom and not much else. What tends to
actually move the needle is finding the cause: reviewing medications with the
prescriber, addressing why you are not breathing through your nose, and in the
meantime using products that add moisture without alcohol, which dries further.

If your mouth has been dry for a long stretch, it is worth knowing which species
took advantage of it while it was.
