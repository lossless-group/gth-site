Thyroid medication gives you T4, a storage form of thyroid hormone. Your body
then has to convert it into T3, the active form that actually does the work in
your cells. A meaningful share of that conversion happens outside the thyroid
gland altogether, and some of it depends on a healthy gut. That is one reason a
perfect TSH can sit next to persistent fatigue.

## Where conversion happens

Most conversion takes place in the liver and kidneys. A smaller portion is
handled with the help of gut bacteria, through an enzyme those bacteria supply.
When the bacterial population thins out, that share of the work slows down. You
are still taking the same dose. Less of it is arriving in usable form.

## The other direction is also true

Low thyroid function slows the gut. Motility drops, transit time stretches, and
bacteria that should be moving along instead sit and multiply where they should
not. That bacterial overgrowth then further interferes with conversion and with
absorption of the iron, selenium and zinc that thyroid function requires.

It is a loop, and loops are why people get stuck. Treating one end while the
other end keeps pulling produces exactly the plateau you are describing.

## What tends to help

Nothing here replaces your medication or your endocrinologist. But when
someone's labs are optimized and they still feel underpowered, it is worth
asking whether the gut is holding up its end: whether transit time is
reasonable, whether nutrient status supports the enzymes involved, and whether
there is a bacterial overgrowth nobody has looked for.

A thyroid panel tells you what is in your blood. It does not tell you what is
happening in the tissue that has to finish the job.
